# AI-isms: research notes

Condensed from a web research pass on 2026-11-15. The patterns in wording, construction, punctuation and storytelling that make text read as written by a large language model. Source for the checklist in `agents/story-review-turing.md`.

Word and phrase lists are tied to model generations and go stale; treat every list here as examples of a kind, not as a complete or permanent set.

## Vocabulary, general

- 2023 to mid-2024 words: delve, tapestry, testament, intricate, interplay, pivotal, crucial, vibrant, meticulously, landscape, underscore, garner, boasts, enduring, realm, embark, robust, seamless, nestled, "Additionally". (Wikipedia; Kobak et al.)
- Later words: align with, enhance, fostering, highlighting, showcasing, leverage, multifaceted, nuanced, myriad, quietly, steady, echoed, matters. (Wikipedia)
- Measured: in 2024 PubMed abstracts the excess words were style words, mostly verbs; "delves" ran 28 times its expected rate. "Delve" has since dropped. (Kobak et al.)
- Filler and importance phrases: "it's worth noting", "when it comes to", "navigating the complexities of", "a testament to", "stands as", "serves as", "a pivotal moment", "setting the stage for", "deeply rooted".
- Vague attribution: "experts argue", "studies show", "some say".
- Hollow intensifiers and hedges: genuinely, truly, "to be honest", "could potentially".
- Therapy and self-help register: "sit with that", "let that sink in", "that matters", "at its core", "carries real weight", "real" as an emphasis word.
- Performed insight: "here's the thing", "the catch?", "turns out", "X is doing a lot of work", "the kind of X that...".

## Vocabulary, fiction

- Breath and body: "her breath hitched", "knuckles turning white", "shivers down her spine", "swallowed hard", "took a deep breath", "skipped a beat", "a single tear", a constant smirk, fingers run through hair.
- Eyes (the largest cluster in community ban lists): eyes that gleam, glint, sparkle, twinkle, soften; "evident in her eyes"; "a smile that did not reach his eyes".
- Stock phrases: "barely above a whisper", "couldn't help but", "despite herself", "a dance of", "a symphony of", "a tapestry of", "felt a mix of", "a pang of", "finds solace in", "casting long shadows", "couldn't shake the feeling", "the weight of", "dimly lit", "cacophony".
- Stock dialogue lines: "you're going to be the death of me", "you know that, right?".
- Spooky and melancholy lexicon: ghosts, echoes, whispers, shadows, humming, flickering, pulsing.
- Reported widely in fiction lists but not confirmed by a source in this pass: "ministrations", "orbs", "ozone", "something shifted", "a breath she didn't know she was holding".

## Sentence-level constructions

- Negative parallelism, the most-cited tell: "not X, but Y", "not just X but Y", "it's not X, it's Y", "no X, no Y, just Z", and multi-sentence versions that knock down an idea nobody raised. In fiction: "The pull from the deep was no current. It was a summons." Estimated at about three times the human rate; reported as the hardest to suppress.
- Colon, then a short fragment staging a revelation the scene has not earned.
- Groups of three: adjectives, nouns or clauses, and three as the default number of anything.
- The short quotable closer: a one-line sentence or paragraph that lands like an aphorism. "Some wounds, she understood now, never fully closed."
- Fragment emphasis: "There it was." "Not even a clever one. Not even hidden."
- Participial tail adding hollow significance: "..., highlighting its importance".
- Reflective gerund opener: "Watching him leave, she realized...".
- Signposting and teasers: "but here's the most unsettling part", rhetorical-question openers.
- Stock transitions: additionally, moreover, furthermore, "that said". Weak alone.
- Synonym cycling: refusing to repeat a word, even where repetition is natural.
- Uniform rhythm: even sentence lengths, or a mechanical alternation of long sentence and tiny one.
- Abstract noun welded to a sensory image: "bruised silence", "grief is infinite recursion".
- Personified objects and weather; weighty metaphors that fall apart when examined; one idea restated in several figures in a row.
- Fake precision: details that sound exact and do nothing.
- Over-explaining what the reader already has.

## Punctuation and typography

- Em dash: the famous marker, now a weak one. Skilled human writers use it; newer models move the same sentence shapes onto semicolons and commas. Density matters, presence does not.
- Colon into fragment, colon into a list of three.
- In running prose: bold for emphasis, bold lead-ins on list items, Title Case headings, emoji, leaked Markdown.
- Stacked hyphenated modifiers.
- Curly quotes and spotless typography are not usable evidence; they depend on the writer's tools.

## Paragraph and document structure

- Every paragraph built the same way: statement, elaboration, example, summing-up line.
- A one-sentence paragraph after each short paragraph, on a beat.
- Summary closers and conclusions that restate the opening.
- Headers and bullets where a person would write a paragraph; lists of bare noun phrases.
- In long fiction: dialogue, exposition and narration in separate blocks; voice and rhythm unchanged across chapters.
- Forced balance: both sides presented where nobody asked.

## Fiction craft

Largest study (StoryScope preprint, more than 50,000 machine stories against about 10,000 human ones): theme stated outright by the narrator in 77% against 52%; dialogue used for philosophical debate 59% against 34%; no subplot 79% against 57%; ending in internal acceptance 47% against 27%; tighter cause-and-effect, less flashback, fewer morally ambiguous choices, fewer specific cultural references. Population-level figures, not a test for one story.

- Emotion as stock body language (tight chest, trembling hands). Human writers in the same data more often simply name the feeling, so swapping one gesture for another does not help.
- Emotion named in a formula: "a wave of grief washed over her", "a complicated mixture of anger and relief".
- Every moment carrying weight; crying and gasping out of proportion.
- Dialogue with no subtext: characters say exactly what they feel; therapy-speak; conflicts resolved like a workplace mediation.
- All characters sounding alike.
- Over-varied speech tags (murmured, breathed, rasped) and adverb or gesture tags on most lines.
- Scene or chapter closing on a line of wisdom or quiet hope; stories that conclude instead of stopping.
- Conflict resolved quickly, in the protagonist's favour, with nothing left open.
- Sensory cataloguing: smell, sound, sight and texture ticked off in turn.
- Heavy-handed recurring motifs.
- Stock characters: the brusque mother with a heart of gold, the cold intellectual, the callous man in law or banking, the loyal bumbling friend, interchangeable children.
- Odd recurring details: knees that crack on standing, events on Thursdays, vague group names such as "The Council".

## Names and default settings

- Cornell study of 20,000 stories from four models: Elias in 26.5% of stories, Mara in 16.7%, Elara in 13.1%; lighthouse, keeper, clockmaker, baker, mayor and librarian among the most repeated words.
- Also reported: Voss, Thorne, Chen (Marcus Chen for a software developer), Lyra, Mira, Maren, Aria, Calder, Fenwick and other "-wick" names, Holloway, Okafor for doctors, Zephyr.
- Places: Eldoria, the Whispering Woods, Ironhaven, Stormwatch, "the Northern Reaches".
- One such name proves nothing; several together are a strong signal.

## Story bibles and outlines

No source studied machine-written story bibles. These are inferences from the story-level findings and should be reported as low-confidence observations:

- Symmetrical character sheets: every character with the same fields at the same length.
- A neat flaw tied to a neat arc for everyone.
- Traits in balanced lists, usually of three.
- Themes written as slogans or one-line morals.
- A single causal chain with no subplot and the resolution already tidy.
- Worlds assembled from familiar defaults; every section at the same level of detail.
- Default names and vague group labels (see above).

## Tone

- Relentless positivity and comfort; hopeful closers whatever the subject.
- Both-sidesing.
- No specific odd detail: statements that would fit many subjects in place of the one true strange fact.
- No individual voice.
- Chatbot residue ("I hope this helps", "Certainly!", unfilled placeholders): rare in polished text, decisive when present.

## Reliability

- Strong alone: chatbot residue and leaked Markdown; several default names or settings together; several stock phrases on one page.
- Meaningful only at density or in clusters: the vocabulary lists, "not X but Y", groups of three, fragments and one-line closers, physical-reaction cliches (decades of genre fiction use them too), the story-shape tells.
- Common in skilled human writing, so weak: the em dash, transition words, tricolons and parallelism as such, clean grammar, plain or formal style, dialogue without subtext (also a novice habit).
- People are unreliable at this: several studies put untrained readers near chance, and even experts sometimes flag human text. Detection software has shown high false-positive rates on non-native writers.
- A human rewrite of a machine draft passes most prose checks while the story shape survives.
- So a finding should say "this reads as machine-patterned" and name the pattern, the count and the place. It should never say a text was written by AI.

## What human-sounding prose has instead

- Plain verbs, "is" and "has", the specific noun for the real thing.
- Uneven rhythm; a word repeated on purpose; a straight statement with no straw man before it.
- Punctuation chosen by sense.
- Paragraphs of uneven build, rough joins, endings that stop.
- Subplots, loose ends, ambiguity, revelations that recast what came before, references to particular things.
- Names and places from the author's own knowledge; checkable, odd, particular detail.
- Opinion, uncertainty, and sentences only this author would write.
